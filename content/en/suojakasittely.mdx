Fabric protection tends to split opinion. Some see it as a pointless add-on, others swear it is the reason their sofa still looks new. The truth sits in between: in the right situation it is genuinely useful, but it is not magic.

## How fabric protection works

The protector is applied as a thin layer that bonds to the individual fibres. It does not form a film over the fabric. Instead it changes the surface tension of the fibres so that liquids and oils do not soak in straight away.

In practice, spilled coffee or juice beads up on the surface for a while instead of going straight into the filling. Dry soil and dust cling to the fibres less, so vacuuming works better. The fabric looks and feels the same as before and still breathes.

## What it does and what it does not

To be blunt: fabric protection buys you time. It is not a force field.

| It does | It does not |
|---|---|
| Slow down how fast liquids absorb | Make the fabric waterproof |
| Give you time to blot a spill before it stains | Prevent stains if a spill is left to dry |
| Reduce how much dirt and dust sticks to fibres | Replace vacuuming and regular cleaning |
| Make the next cleaning easier | Protect against wear, pilling or cat claws |
| Slow down greying on armrests | Fully stop dye transfer from jeans |

If a glass of red wine is blotted within a minute, protected fabric will most likely be fine. If it is only noticed the next morning, a stain will probably remain, although it is usually easier to remove.

<Callout type="tip" title="Blot, do not rub">
Protection works best when you blot a spill immediately with a clean, light-coloured cloth. Rubbing pushes liquid between the fibres and wears the protective layer.
</Callout>

## When it is worth it

- **Light fabrics.** On white, beige or pale grey, every small spill shows.
- **Families with children.** Juice, yoghurt and felt-tip pens are part of life. Protection will not stop everything, but it gives you time.
- **Pets.** Paw prints, drool and the dirt carried in fur stick less to treated fabric.
- **A new sofa.** The fabric is clean, so the protector bonds well.
- **A freshly cleaned sofa.** The ideal moment: dirt and old residue are gone and the protector goes onto a clean surface.

It makes less sense for a dark, patterned fabric in a room that is rarely used.

## How long it lasts

Mostly it depends on use. Seats and armrests lose protection faster than the back of the sofa, and cleaning also reduces it.

A practical rule is to renew the treatment after every deep clean. For a busy family sofa that usually means about once a year; with lighter use, less often. You can tell it has worn off when a drop of water no longer beads up but soaks in straight away.

## DIY spray or professional application

Shop-bought fabric protector sprays do work to a degree. The difference is in the application:

- **Even coverage.** The spray should go on evenly everywhere, neither too wet nor too thin. At home it is easy to miss patches or overdo others.
- **A clean base.** Protector sprayed over dirt locks the dirt into the fibres. A professional applies it straight after cleaning.
- **The right product.** Not every spray suits every fabric. The wrong one can leave marks or change the feel.
- **Safety.** You need good ventilation and should not breathe in the mist.

<Callout type="warning" title="Always test first">
If you apply a spray yourself, try it on a hidden spot and let it dry completely. Do not use a fabric spray on leather unless it is specifically made for leather.
</Callout>

## In short

- Fabric protection slows down how fast liquids and dirt soak in, but it does not make fabric waterproof.
- It pays off most for light fabrics, homes with kids or pets, and new or freshly cleaned sofas.
- You still need to blot spills straight away.
- Renew it after each deep clean, roughly once a year with heavy use.
- The best result comes from even application on clean fabric.
